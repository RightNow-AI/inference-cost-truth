A language model bills you for tokens, not for words or characters. The mapping between them is set by the tokenizer that ships with the model, and it is not the same across vendors. A paragraph of ordinary English prose may cost one provider fifteen percent more tokens than another provider for exactly the same input text, before any difference in the advertised price per million tokens is taken into account. Code, markup, and non-Latin scripts widen that gap further, because vocabularies are trained on different corpus mixtures and allocate their merges differently. Anyone comparing two published per-token prices without first normalising for tokenizer behaviour is comparing two numbers that are denominated in different units.
